## When Do I Record Revenue? — Sales, Income, Earnings, Revenue Recognition

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Recognize revenue when or as the company satisfies its performance obligations."

Revenue is generally recorded when control of promised goods or services transfers to the customer, rather than simply when cash is received. This means a company may recognize revenue before receiving payment. For example, if a company delivers a $2,000 product on March 28 but receives payment on April 15, it ordinarily recognizes the $2,000 revenue in March. The timing of revenue recognition depends on when the company satisfies its performance obligations.

Reference: ASC 606 — Revenue from Contracts with Customers.

## When Do I Record Expenses? — Costs, Bills, Expense Matching

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Recognize expenses in the appropriate accounting period, regardless of when cash is paid."

Under accrual accounting, expenses are recorded when incurred or as their associated benefits are consumed. This means the timing of an expense does not necessarily match the timing of its payment. For example, if employees work in December but receive their wages in January, the company generally records the wage expense in December. Some costs, such as inventory, are expensed when the related goods are sold. Therefore, the appropriate accounting period depends on when the expense is incurred or when the associated benefits are consumed.

Reference: ASC 710 — Compensation; ASC 330 — Inventory.

## What Is Accrual Accounting? — Cash Basis, Earned Revenue, Unpaid Expenses

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Record economic transactions in the periods they affect, not merely when cash changes hands."

Financial statements generally use accrual accounting rather than cash-basis accounting. Under accrual accounting, transactions are recognized in the periods they affect, even when the related cash has not yet been received or paid. This includes recognizing receivables for revenue that has been earned but not yet collected. It also includes recognizing liabilities for expenses that have been incurred but remain unpaid. The goal of accrual accounting is to represent the company's economic activity accurately for each accounting period rather than reporting transactions only when cash changes hands.

Reference: ASC 105 — Generally Accepted Accounting Principles; ASC 606; ASC 710.

## What Is the Accounting Equation? — Assets, Liabilities, Owner's Equity

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Total assets equal total liabilities plus total equity."

The balance sheet must satisfy the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. This equation describes the relationship between a company's assets, liabilities, and equity. For example, if a company owns $100,000 in assets and owes $40,000 to creditors, its equity is $60,000. The $100,000 in assets equals the combined $40,000 in liabilities and $60,000 in equity. Every properly recorded transaction preserves this equality. The accounting equation must remain balanced as the company records transactions affecting its financial position.

Reference: ASC 210 — Balance Sheet; FASB Concepts Statement No. 8.

## What Is the Cost Principle? — Purchase Price, Asset Value, Historical Cost

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Initially measure many purchased assets using their acquisition cost."

Many assets are initially recorded at their purchase cost. This initial recorded amount can include qualifying costs necessary to prepare the asset for use. For example, if equipment costs $15,000 and installation costs $2,000, its initial recorded cost may be $17,000. This illustrates how acquisition cost can include more than the asset's purchase price alone. However, GAAP also requires fair-value or other measurements for certain assets and transactions. Therefore, acquisition cost is an important initial measurement basis for many assets, but it is not the required measurement basis for every asset or transaction.

Reference: ASC 360 — Property, Plant, and Equipment; ASC 820 — Fair Value Measurement.

## How Does Depreciation Work? — Equipment, Useful Life, Asset Expense

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Allocate the depreciable cost of a long-lived tangible asset systematically over its useful life."

Depreciation spreads the cost of equipment or buildings over the periods benefiting from their use. Rather than treating the entire depreciable cost as an expense in a single period, depreciation allocates that cost systematically over the asset's useful life. For example, equipment costing $20,000 with a five-year useful life and no estimated salvage value may generate $4,000 in annual straight-line depreciation expense. This example illustrates how an asset's cost can be allocated across multiple accounting periods. Depreciation is associated with long-lived tangible assets and the periods that benefit from their use.

Reference: ASC 360 — Property, Plant, and Equipment.

## How Do I Value Inventory? — Unsold Products, Merchandise, Stock Cost

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Generally report inventory at the lower of cost and net realizable value."

Under most inventory costing methods, inventory is written down when its net realizable value falls below cost. For example, if inventory costs $8,000 but its net realizable value falls to $6,500, the company generally reports the inventory at $6,500 and recognizes a $1,500 loss. This reflects the difference between the inventory's original cost and its lower net realizable value. However, inventory measured using LIFO or the retail inventory method has different lower-of-cost-or-market guidance. Therefore, the applicable inventory costing method matters when determining how inventory is valued and whether a write-down is required.

Reference: ASC 330 — Inventory.

## What Happens When a Customer Doesn't Pay? — Bad Debt, Uncollectible Accounts

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Estimate expected credit losses on receivables subject to the credit-loss standard."

Companies generally establish an allowance for expected uncollectible receivables rather than waiting until every unpaid account is confirmed worthless. This means expected credit losses can be recognized before the company determines that a particular receivable will not be collected. For example, if a company has $100,000 of receivables and expects $3,000 in credit losses, it records an allowance of $3,000. The allowance represents the expected uncollectible amount. This approach focuses on estimating expected credit losses for receivables subject to the applicable credit-loss standard.

Reference: ASC 326 — Financial Instruments—Credit Losses.

## What Is a Prepaid Expense? — Advance Payment, Insurance, Rent

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Recognize a qualifying advance payment as an asset and expense it as the benefit is received."

A prepaid expense involves a qualifying payment made in advance of receiving the related benefit. Instead of recognizing the entire payment as an expense immediately, the company initially records it as an asset and recognizes expenses as the benefits are received. For example, if a company pays $12,000 for 12 months of insurance coverage, it initially records $12,000 in prepaid insurance as an asset. Under a uniform coverage pattern, it then recognizes $1,000 of insurance expense each month. This allocates the insurance cost to the periods receiving the coverage.

Reference: ASC 340 — Other Assets and Deferred Costs.

## Is Money Received in Advance Revenue? — Deposits, Customer Prepayments, Unearned Revenue

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Record customer payments received before satisfying performance obligations as a contract liability."

Receiving cash does not always mean a company has earned revenue. When a customer pays before the company satisfies its performance obligations, the payment is initially recorded as a contract liability. For example, if a customer pays $600 in advance for six monthly services, the company initially records a $600 liability. Assuming each service has equal value, the company recognizes $100 of revenue each month as the services are provided. This illustrates the distinction between receiving payment and earning revenue. Revenue recognition occurs as the company satisfies the relevant performance obligations.

Reference: ASC 606 — Revenue from Contracts with Customers.

## What Is the Going Concern Assumption? — Business Survival, Bankruptcy, Continuing Operations

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Evaluate whether substantial doubt exists about the company's ability to continue as a going concern."

Financial reporting ordinarily assumes that a business will continue operating. Management must evaluate whether conditions raise substantial doubt about the company's ability to meet its obligations within one year after the financial statements are issued or available to be issued. This evaluation addresses whether the company can continue operating and meeting its obligations during the relevant period. The disclosures required in the financial statements depend on the results of management's evaluation and management's plans. Therefore, going concern considerations involve both the company's circumstances and the assessment performed by management.

Reference: ASC 205-40 — Going Concern.

## What Must a Company Disclose? — Footnotes, Financial Transparency, Important Information

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Provide disclosures required by applicable accounting standards to help users understand the financial statements."

Financial statements often require explanatory notes that provide additional information about a company's financial reporting. These notes may cover accounting policies, commitments, risks, and other significant matters. For example, a company may need to disclose material litigation contingencies, borrowing arrangements, and significant accounting estimates. These disclosures provide information that helps users understand the financial statements and matters affecting the company. The specific disclosures required depend on the applicable accounting standards and the circumstances being reported. Financial statement notes therefore serve an important role in communicating information beyond the amounts presented in the financial statements.

Reference: ASC 235 — Notes to Financial Statements; ASC 450 — Contingencies.

## Can a Company Change Accounting Methods? — Consistency, Comparability, Policy Changes

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Apply accounting principles consistently and justify changes when a different accounting principle is adopted."

Companies should use accounting methods consistently so that financial results are comparable across reporting periods. Consistency helps users compare financial information from one period with information from another. However, a company may make a voluntary change in accounting principle. Such a change generally requires the company to establish that the alternative accounting principle is preferable. The company must also apply the relevant transition and disclosure requirements. Therefore, accounting methods are generally expected to remain consistent, while changes in accounting principles are subject to requirements concerning preferability, transition, and disclosure.

Reference: ASC 250 — Accounting Changes and Error Corrections.

## When Do I Record a Lawsuit Loss? — Contingent Liabilities, Legal Claims, Possible Losses

GAAP Rule (paraphrased): "Accrue an estimated loss contingency when loss is probable and reasonably estimable."

If a company faces a lawsuit and a loss is probable and can be reasonably estimated, it generally records both a liability and an expense. For example, a probable settlement that can be reasonably estimated at $50,000 typically requires the company to record a $50,000 liability. However, if the loss is reasonably possible rather than probable, disclosure may be required without recording a liability. The distinction between a probable loss and a reasonably possible loss is therefore important when determining whether a lawsuit-related loss must be accrued or disclosed.

Reference: ASC 450-20 — Loss Contingencies.

## What Is Materiality? — Small Errors, Significant Amounts, Financial Statement Misstatements

GAAP Concept (paraphrased): "Information is material when its omission or misstatement could influence users' decisions."

Materiality helps determine whether financial information is important enough to affect the decisions of financial statement users. The significance of an error depends on more than its numerical amount. For example, a $100 error may be insignificant to a multibillion-dollar company but significant to a very small business. This demonstrates how the same amount can have different levels of importance depending on the company's circumstances. Both the size and nature of an error matter when evaluating materiality. Information is considered material when omitting or misstating it could influence the decisions of financial statement users.

Reference: FASB Concepts Statement No. 8; applicable SEC materiality guidance for public companies.
